Gerund vs. Infinitive

A complete grammar reference for B2 students — rules, explanations, and examples

English has two non-finite verb forms that often follow another verb, adjective, or preposition: the gerund (the -ing form acting like a noun, e.g. swimming) and the infinitive (to + base verb, e.g. to swim, or sometimes the bare form without to). Which one to use depends mainly on the word that comes before it. This reference works through every pattern in order, from simple to complex.
1

Verbs followed ONLY by the gerund

verb + -ing

Some verbs are always followed by a gerund, never an infinitive, when another action follows them directly. Many of these verbs describe an attitude toward an action (liking, disliking, avoiding it), a decision to stop or delay something, or an action that is already underway or habitual. Because the gerund behaves like a noun, you can think of it as "the activity of doing X" rather than a plan to do it in the future.

Common verbs: enjoy, avoid, consider, suggest, mind, finish, deny, admit, risk, practise, imagine, recall, postpone, delay, resist, resume, quit, miss, keep (on), can't help, give up, put off, carry on, involve, appreciate, dread, fancy, defer
  • She avoided answering the question. (she didn't answer — a completed avoidance)
  • I can't help feeling nervous before exams. (an ongoing, uncontrollable feeling)
  • They suggested postponing the meeting.
  • He denied stealing the money.
Memory tip: most of these verbs are about judging, avoiding, or continuing an activity — not about planning or intending to do something new.
2

Verbs followed ONLY by the infinitive

verb + to + base verb

Other verbs are always followed by an infinitive. These typically express a decision, intention, hope, or future plan — something that has not happened yet and that the subject is aiming toward. Because the infinitive points forward in time (toward a goal or result), it fits naturally after verbs of wanting, deciding, and promising.

Common verbs: want, decide, hope, plan, agree, promise, refuse, manage, afford, offer, seem, appear, tend, fail, learn, arrange, expect, intend, choose, pretend, demand, aim, long, swear, threaten, volunteer, claim
  • We decided to leave early. (a decision made — then the future action)
  • He refused to apologise.
  • She managed to finish on time. (the result of effort)
  • They agreed to help us move.
Memory tip: picture the infinitive's "to" as an arrow pointing toward a future goal — that's exactly what these verbs are about.
3

Verbs followed by either, with NO change in meaning

A small group of verbs can take either form with essentially the same meaning. This usually happens with verbs describing the beginning, continuation, or general liking/disliking of an activity — the action itself is more important than whether it's framed as an ongoing activity (gerund) or a goal (infinitive).

Verbs: begin, start, continue, like, love, hate, prefer, intend, bother
  • It started raining / started to rain. (identical meaning)
  • I like swimming / like to swim in the mornings.
  • She hates waiting / hates to wait for buses.
Exception: when the main verb is already in a continuous tense, avoid stacking two -ing forms. Say "It's starting to rain," not "It's starting raining."
4

Verbs followed by either, with a CHANGE in meaning

This is the most important — and most tested — group at B2 level. With these verbs, choosing the gerund or the infinitive genuinely changes what the sentence means, so students need to understand the logic behind each pair rather than memorise them as a fixed list.

Verb+ gerund means…+ infinitive means…
remember recalling something that already happened: I remember locking the door. (I have a memory of the action) not forgetting to do a task: I remembered to lock the door. (I did it because I didn't forget)
forget I'll never forget seeing the pyramids. (a memory that will stay with you) Don't forget to call me. (a task you need to carry out)
stop He stopped smoking. (the gerund is the activity that is now stopped — he no longer smokes) He stopped to smoke. (he paused a different activity in order to smoke)
try Try restarting the computer. (an experiment — do this and see if it solves the problem) I tried to open the door. (a genuine effort, possibly unsuccessful)
regret I regret telling her. (sorrow about a past action) I regret to inform you that... (formal — regret about what you're about to say)
mean Winning this contract would mean working weekends. (would involve/result in) I meant to call you yesterday. (intended but didn't necessarily do it)
go on She went on talking about the weather. (continued the same topic/activity) She went on to talk about the economy. (moved on to a new, different activity)
need The car needs washing. (passive sense — the car needs to be washed by someone) I need to wash the car. (active sense — I personally need to do it)
Teaching tip: for each pair, ask students "is this about a memory/past event, or about a task/future action?" That question resolves remember, forget, and regret instantly.
5

used to / be used to / get used to

These three look similar but belong to completely different grammar categories, which is why they're grouped separately from the pairs above. Used to is about past habits and takes the infinitive. Be used to and get used to use "used to" as an adjective phrase (meaning "accustomed"), and since "to" here is a preposition, they're followed by a gerund.

  • used to + infinitive — a past habit that no longer happens: I used to smoke. (I don't smoke now)
  • be used to + gerund — accustomed to something in the present: I'm used to getting up early. (it doesn't bother me anymore)
  • get used to + gerund — the process of becoming accustomed: I'm getting used to the new schedule. (still adjusting)
6

Verb + object + infinitive

verb + person/thing + to + base verb

Many verbs of asking, telling, or persuading are followed by an object (the person being asked/told) and then an infinitive describing what that person should do. The object is essential — these verbs describe one person directing or enabling another person's action.

Common verbs: want, ask, tell, advise, allow, encourage, expect, invite, order, persuade, remind, warn, force, get, teach, would like, permit
  • She asked him to wait.
  • They encouraged her to apply.
  • Don't forget to remind me to call him. (remind + object + to-infinitive)
  • The teacher allowed us to leave early.
7

Verb + object + bare infinitive (no "to")

Three common causative verbs — make, let, and help — are followed by an object and the base verb with no "to" when used in the active voice. This is an irregular but very frequent pattern, so it's worth practising on its own.

  • My boss made me redo the report. (forced)
  • Let me explain. (permission)
  • Can you help me carry this? ("help" can also take "to": help me to carry)
Passive exception: in the passive voice, "make" switches back to the full infinitive — He was made to redo it.
8

Verbs of perception + object + bare infinitive or -ing

With verbs like see, hear, watch, feel, and notice, the choice of form changes how much of the action you witnessed. The bare infinitive describes the complete action from start to finish, while the -ing form describes the action already in progress, or only part of it observed.

  • I saw him cross the street. (I watched the whole crossing, start to finish)
  • I saw him crossing the street (as I drove past). (I only caught part of the action, already in progress)
  • We heard her sing the whole song. (the complete performance)
  • We heard her singing as we walked past the window. (just a moment of it)
9

Preposition + gerund

This is one of the most reliable rules in English grammar: a verb that directly follows a preposition is always a gerund, never an infinitive — even when the preposition happens to be the word "to." This matters because students often confuse the infinitive marker "to" with the preposition "to," which looks identical but behaves differently (compare I want to go with I'm looking forward to going).

Verb + preposition + -ing: look forward to, insist on, succeed in, believe in, think about, worry about, apologise for, be responsible for, object to, get used to, adapt to, accuse (someone) of
Adjective + preposition + -ing: interested in, good/bad at, afraid of, tired of, keen on, capable of, famous for, fond of, worried about, excited about
Noun + preposition + -ing: an interest in, a reason for, the idea of, an excuse for, a chance of
  • I'm looking forward to seeing you. ("to" here is a preposition, not an infinitive marker)
  • He insisted on paying the bill.
  • She's interested in learning Japanese.
  • There's no excuse for being late.
10

Gerund as the subject of a sentence

When an activity itself is the topic of the sentence — the thing being discussed, rather than something a particular person is planning to do — English normally places it at the start as a gerund. An infinitive subject is grammatically possible but sounds formal and old-fashioned by comparison, so gerund subjects are the natural, everyday choice.

  • Smoking is bad for your health.
  • Learning a language takes time.
  • Eating late at night can disrupt your sleep.
More formal alternative: To learn a language takes time. Grammatically correct, but much less common in everyday speech or writing.
11

Infinitive of purpose ("in order to")

When you explain why someone does something — the goal or reason behind an action — use the infinitive, not the gerund. This is sometimes called the "infinitive of purpose," and it can be made more formal and explicit with "in order to" or "so as to."

  • I went to the shop to buy milk.
  • She studies hard (in order) to pass the exam.
  • In order not to be late, he left early. (negative purpose — "not" goes before "to")
  • He whispered so as not to wake the baby.
12

Adjective + infinitive

It's + adjective + (of/for + person) + to + base verb

After many adjectives describing feelings, judgments, or necessity, English uses an infinitive to explain what caused that feeling or what needs to happen. A frequent point of confusion is whether to use "of" or "for" before the person — the rule depends on whether the adjective describes a personal quality or a general situation.

  • It was nice to meet you.
  • It's important to arrive on time.
  • It was kind of you to help. ("kind" describes a personal quality → use "of")
  • It's difficult for beginners to understand. ("difficult" describes the situation, not the person → use "for")
Rule of thumb: use of with adjectives that judge someone's character (kind, nice, stupid, rude, generous, silly). Use for with adjectives describing difficulty, necessity, or general conditions (difficult, important, necessary, easy, possible).
13

too / enough + infinitive

Too and enough both describe a degree that leads to a result, and that result is expressed with an infinitive. The two words sit in different positions relative to the adjective or noun: "too" comes before the adjective, while "enough" comes after an adjective but before a noun.

  • too + adjective + infinitive: This coffee is too hot to drink. (so hot that I can't drink it)
  • adjective + enough + infinitive: He's old enough to vote. (sufficiently old)
  • enough + noun + infinitive: We don't have enough time to finish. (note: "enough" goes before the noun here)
14

Complex / advanced forms (perfect, passive, continuous)

Beyond the simple -ing and to-infinitive forms, both the gerund and infinitive can be built in perfect, continuous, and passive versions. These let you show that an action happened earlier than the main verb, is still ongoing, or is being done to the subject rather than by it. B2 students should be able to recognise and interpret these even if they use the simple forms most often in speech.

  • He denied having stolen the money. (perfect gerund — the stealing happened before the denial)
  • I'm glad to have finished the project. (perfect infinitive — relief about something already completed)

  • He hates being told what to do. (passive gerund)

Quick decision checklist for students

  1. Is it right after a preposition (including "to" used as a preposition)? → use the gerund.
  2. Is it right after a verb from one of the lists above? → check whether that verb is gerund-only, infinitive-only, or a meaning-change verb.
  3. Is it explaining why someone does something (purpose)? → use the infinitive.
  4. Is it the subject of the sentence? → usually the gerund.
  5. Does it follow make / let / help + an object? → use the bare infinitive (no "to").
  6. Does it follow a verb of perception (see, hear, watch) + an object? → bare infinitive for the whole action, -ing for an action already in progress.
